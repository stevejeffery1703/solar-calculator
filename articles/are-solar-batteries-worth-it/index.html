Are Solar Batteries Worth It in 2026?

Adding a battery to a home solar system lets you store the energy your panels produce during the day and use it at night or during an outage — instead of sending the excess to the grid. Whether that is worth the extra cost depends on your goals, your utility's rules, and how often your power goes out.

What a home battery costs

In 2026, a home solar battery typically costs $9,000 to $18,000 installed, or roughly $800 to $1,300 per usable kilowatt-hour (kWh) of storage. A single battery unit of around 10 to 13.5 kWh usually lands near $11,500 to $16,500 fully installed. The battery itself is only part of the price — the rest goes to the inverter, wiring, labor, and permitting.

Note that the 30% federal tax credit that used to offset battery costs for homeowners ended on December 31, 2025, so a battery you buy in 2026 no longer qualifies for it. See solar incentives in 2026 for what remains.

When a battery is worth it

  • You get frequent outages. A battery keeps essential circuits running when the grid goes down — often the main reason homeowners add one.
  • Your utility pays little for exported power. Where net metering is weak, storing and using your own energy is worth more than exporting it for a small credit.
  • You have time-of-use rates. If electricity costs more in the evening, a battery lets you use stored daytime energy instead of buying expensive peak power.
  • You want more energy independence or plan to go off-grid.

When it may not pay off

  • You have strong (1:1) net metering. If your utility credits exported energy at the full retail rate, the grid already acts like a "free battery," and a physical one adds cost without adding much saving.
  • Outages are rare and backup power is not a priority.
  • Your budget is tight. A battery lengthens the payback period, because it is a sizeable extra cost that does not always pay for itself on energy savings alone.

The bottom line

For backup power and peace of mind, many homeowners feel a battery is worth it regardless of the strict math. As a pure investment, it makes the most sense where net metering is weak or outages are common. If your main goal is simply to cut your bill and you have good net metering, the panels usually do the heavy lifting on their own.
